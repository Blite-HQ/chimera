# Notes — Class 04

## Resumen

First technical session introducing quantum systems from a CS perspective, built by directly contrasting each classical construct from c03 with its quantum counterpart. Introduces the qubit as an amplitude vector, the Born rule connecting amplitudes to probabilities, superposition, unitary transformations (replacing stochastic matrices), Dirac/ket notation, the Hadamard gate and basis, the classical-vs-quantum double-flip experiment showing interference, tensor products of qubits, separability vs. entanglement, and the class BQP.

## Conceptos clave

- **Only two rules needed from quantum physics** [~00:00]: (1) system state is described by a vector of amplitudes (arbitrary complex numbers, restricted to real numbers for the first sessions) rather than a probability vector; (2) the Born rule connects amplitudes to probabilities.
- **Qubit as amplitude vector** [~00:00]: analogous to the classical bit's probability vector p·[1,0] + (1-p)·[0,1], a qubit is written α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ = [α, β], where α, β are amplitudes (here restricted to real numbers) rather than probabilities — so they can be negative.
- **Born rule** [~00:00]: probability of observing outcome 0 is α², probability of observing outcome 1 is β² (for real amplitudes); requires α² + β² = 1.
- **Superposition vs. convex combination** [~00:00]: because α, β can be negative, α|0⟩+β|1⟩ is not a convex combination (that required non-negative weights) — this new kind of decomposition is called superposition.
- **Superposition ≠ "in both states at once"** [~00:00–20:00]: the instructor explicitly warns against the popular-science interpretation that a qubit is "in state 0 and 1 at the same time," drawing a direct analogy to the classical probability vector — nobody says a classical coin is "in heads and tails at the same time" just because its probability vector has two nonzero entries. The amplitude/superposition description is treated as a richer mathematical formalism, not evidence of literal simultaneous existence in two states; this is presented as the instructor's own pragmatic stance among competing quantum interpretations, not a settled fact.
- **Unit-circle visualization** [~00:00–20:00]: with real amplitudes, α=cos θ, β=sin θ, θ∈[0,2π) — a qubit is any point on the unit circle, versus the classical probability vector being restricted to a straight line segment between [1,0] and [0,1]. Full complex amplitudes require the Bloch sphere (not needed for early sessions).
- **More than 2 basis states** [~20:00]: a 3-level system generalizes to a 3-dimensional superposition over 3 basis vectors; same idea extends to n levels.
- **Quantum transformations = unitary matrices** [~20:00]: the quantum analogue of a stochastic matrix; a transformation is valid iff it maps every valid quantum state (Σα_i²=1) to another valid quantum state. Equivalent characterizations (real-entry case): UᵀU = I (orthogonal matrix); Uᵀ = U⁻¹ (reversible/invertible); with complex entries, U†U = I (conjugate transpose). Unitary evolution models a closed system — always reversible, no information loss.
- **Hermitian matrices** [~20:00]: mentioned in passing as a second important class (H† = H) relevant later in the course, not used yet.
- **Dirac / ket notation** [~20:00]: |0⟩ = [1,0]ᵀ, |1⟩ = [0,1]ᵀ; α|0⟩+β|1⟩ is shorthand for the same column vector; kets can carry informative labels (e.g. |ψ⟩, |φ⟩); bra ⟨φ| is the transpose (row-vector) version; inner product ⟨φ|ψ⟩ computed by distributing over basis kets, using ⟨0|1⟩=0, ⟨0|0⟩=⟨1|1⟩=1 (orthonormal computational/standard basis). For n-dimensional systems, basis labels generalize (e.g. |2⟩ = [0,0,1]ᵀ in 3D) and are especially useful for writing sparse states (2ⁿ-dimensional vectors with few nonzero entries) compactly.
- **Hadamard matrix H** [~20:00–40:00]: H = (1/√2)[[1,1],[1,-1]]; verified unitary (HᵀH=I, and H is also symmetric H=Hᵀ). H|0⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩+|1⟩) = the **plus state** |+⟩; H|1⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩-|1⟩) = the **minus state** |−⟩. These form the **Hadamard basis**, an alternative basis (rotated by angle θ relative to the computational basis) that also spans the 2D space — any angle θ gives a valid basis, not just the computational and Hadamard ones.
- **Contrast with classical fair-coin matrix F** [~40:00]: F=(1/2)[[1,1],[1,1]] (from c03) vs. H — same structural role (applied to a basis vector, produces a uniform-looking outcome) but two key quantum distinctions: (1) normalization uses the L2/Euclidean norm (Σα_i²=1, hence 1/√2) rather than the L1 norm (Σp_i=1, hence 1/2); (2) H has a negative entry, impossible for F since F's entries are literal probabilities.
- **Classical double-flip experiment (no measurement in between)** [~40:00]: starting at |1,0⟩ (heads), applying F once gives (1/2,1/2); applying F twice in a row (no observation between) still gives (1/2,1/2), since (1/2,1/2) is a fixed point of F. Interpreted as: flipping the coin twice without looking still leaves you with the same uniform uncertainty at the end.
- **Classical double-flip WITH measurement in between** [~40:00]: observing the coin after the first flip doesn't change the final statistics — final state is still (1/2,1/2) regardless of whether you looked in between, because observation only updates the observer's knowledge, not the physical outcome distribution.
- **Quantum double-Hadamard experiment (no measurement in between)** [~40:00–60:00]: starting at |0⟩, applying H gives |+⟩; applying H again gives back |0⟩ exactly (deterministically) — because H is self-inverse (H²=I) and the cancellation of the ± signs is what produces this deterministic collapse-free result. This is qualitatively different from the classical case: repeating the "quantum coin flip" twice without measuring produces a certain outcome, not a 50/50 mix.
- **Quantum double-Hadamard WITH measurement in between** [~60:00]: measuring after the first Hadamard collapses |+⟩ into 0 or 1 with 50/50 probability (matching classical statistics); applying H to whichever collapsed state resulted (|0⟩ or |1⟩) regenerates superposition (|+⟩ or |−⟩) rather than canceling deterministically — matching the classical case's final 50/50 uncertainty. The deterministic cancellation is destroyed by the act of measurement, since measurement updates the state, not just the observer's knowledge (unlike the classical case) — this pair of experiments is the session's core illustration of a genuine quantum-vs-classical behavioral difference, attributed to the negative sign / interference in the Hadamard transform.
- **Tensor product of qubits** [~60:00]: identical construction to c03's classical tensor product, just in ket notation: |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩ where ψ=α|0⟩+β|1⟩, φ=γ|0⟩+δ|1⟩ gives αγ|00⟩+αδ|01⟩+βγ|10⟩+βδ|11⟩ — same 4 amplitudes as the classical joint vector, with kets like |00⟩ doubling as both a label and (via Dirac notation) an implicit index into the vector.
- **Separable states and efficient classical simulation** [~60:00–80:00]: an n-qubit state is called separable if it factors as a tensor product of n individual single-qubit states. Key argument: if a quantum circuit's state remains separable throughout its evolution, only the n individual amplitudes (α_i per qubit) need to be tracked and updated — O(n) memory and polynomial-time update — giving an efficient classical simulation. Complex amplitudes just double the memory (2 reals per amplitude), not a qualitative change.
- **Entanglement (preview, formally defined next session)** [~80:00]: a quantum state that is NOT separable (cannot be written as a tensor product of individual qubit states) is called entangled — directly analogous to c03's non-decomposable classical joint probability vectors. Any quantum algorithm that cannot be efficiently classically simulated must, at some point in its evolution, pass through a non-separable (entangled) state — i.e., interesting quantum computation requires creating correlations across qubits.
- **A named theorem broadening the simulability class (name garbled in transcript, likely mis-transcribed)** [~80:00]: separability is a sufficient but not necessary condition for efficient classical simulability — a broader class of states, including some entangled ones, is also known to be efficiently classically simulable; the exact boundary of classical-simulability vs. not is an open research question.
- **BQP defined precisely** [~80:00]: Bounded-error Quantum Polynomial time — the quantum analogue of classical BPP (Bounded-error Probabilistic Polynomial time); "bounded" refers to the computation's error being bounded. Clarification: being in BQP does not imply efficient classical simulability (that would require being in P) — e.g. Shor's algorithm is in BQP but not known to be in P.
- **Roadmap** [~80:00]: tomorrow's session will formally define quantum entanglement, explore quantum protocols/non-classical results enabled by entanglement, and give first hands-on Qiskit circuit examples.

## Fórmulas y definiciones

- **Qubit**: |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ = [α, β]ᵀ, α,β real (extended to complex later), with α²+β²=1. [~00:00]
- **Born rule (real-amplitude case)**: P(observe 0) = α², P(observe 1) = β². [~00:00]
- **Unit-circle parametrization**: α = cos θ, β = sin θ, θ ∈ [0, 2π). [~00:00–20:00]
- **Unitary transformation conditions (real entries)**: UᵀU = I ⟺ Uᵀ = U⁻¹ ⟺ U invertible/reversible. (Complex entries: U†U = I, with † = conjugate transpose.) [~20:00]
- **Ket/bra notation**: |0⟩=[1,0]ᵀ, |1⟩=[0,1]ᵀ; ⟨φ| = |φ⟩ᵀ (or conjugate-transpose for complex); ⟨0|0⟩=⟨1|1⟩=1, ⟨0|1⟩=⟨1|0⟩=0 (orthonormal computational basis); ⟨φ|ψ⟩ = αγ + βδ for ψ=α|0⟩+β|1⟩, φ=γ|0⟩+δ|1⟩. [~20:00]
- **Hadamard matrix**: H = (1/√2)[[1, 1], [1, −1]]; H|0⟩ = |+⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩+|1⟩); H|1⟩ = |−⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩−|1⟩); H is unitary and symmetric (H=Hᵀ), self-inverse (H²=I). [~20:00–40:00]
- **Classical fair-coin matrix (recap from c03)**: F = (1/2)[[1,1],[1,1]]; F applied to any probability vector yields (1/2, 1/2), a fixed point. [~40:00]
- **Tensor product (ket form)**: (α|0⟩+β|1⟩) ⊗ (γ|0⟩+δ|1⟩) = αγ|00⟩ + αδ|01⟩ + βγ|10⟩ + βδ|11⟩. [~60:00]
- **Separable n-qubit state**: |ψ⟩ = ⊗ᵢ (αᵢ|0⟩+βᵢ|1⟩) — tensor product of n single-qubit states; tracked with O(n) memory/updates for classical simulation. [~60:00–80:00]
- **BQP**: Bounded-error Quantum Polynomial time — quantum analogue of classical BPP. [~80:00]

## Glosario

- **Amplitude**: complex (here real) number entry of a quantum state vector; probability = amplitude² (real case) via the Born rule. [~00:00]
- **Superposition**: decomposition of a quantum state into basis vectors with amplitude coefficients, generalizing convex combination to allow negative (or complex) coefficients. [~00:00]
- **Born rule**: rule converting amplitudes into observation probabilities (square the amplitude, for real amplitudes). [~00:00]
- **Unitary matrix**: quantum analogue of a stochastic matrix; U†U = I; always reversible; models closed-system evolution. [~20:00]
- **Hermitian matrix**: matrix equal to its own conjugate transpose (H†=H); introduced as a preview, used later in the course. [~20:00]
- **Ket / bra**: Dirac notation for column vectors (|ψ⟩) and row vectors (⟨ψ|). [~20:00]
- **Computational / standard basis**: the basis {|0⟩, |1⟩} (and higher-dimensional analogues). [~20:00]
- **Hadamard basis**: the basis {|+⟩, |−⟩} produced by applying H to {|0⟩, |1⟩}. [~20:00–40:00]
- **Separable state**: an n-qubit state expressible as a tensor product of n single-qubit states; efficiently classically simulable. [~60:00–80:00]
- **Entangled state**: a quantum state that is not separable; preview of tomorrow's formal topic. [~80:00]
- **BQP (Bounded-error Quantum Polynomial time)**: complexity class of problems solvable in polynomial time on a quantum computer with bounded error; quantum analogue of BPP. [~80:00]
- **BPP (Bounded-error Probabilistic Polynomial time)**: classical complexity class referenced as BQP's classical analogue. [~80:00]
