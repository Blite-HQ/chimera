# Notes — Class 02 (Prospects of Quantum Computing)

## Resumen

A big-picture, mostly non-technical lecture ("Prospects of Quantum Computing") covering: quantum information as a field broader than quantum computing; Feynman's motivation for quantum computers; classical complexity theory foundations needed to understand quantum computing's promise (Turing machines, Church-Turing thesis, P/NP/NP-complete); Shor's algorithm and the class BQP; the NISQ era and the ongoing, non-permanent "race" for quantum advantage; and the open problem of verifying quantum computation output when it falls outside NP.

## Conceptos clave

- **Quantum information ⊃ quantum computing** [~00:00]: quantum computing is one subfield of the broader field of applying quantum physics to information-theoretic problems, which also includes quantum sensing, quantum metrology, and quantum communication.
- **Historical precedents predating "quantum computing"** [~00:00]: Stephen Wiesner's quantum money proposal (late 1970s); BB84 quantum key distribution (Bennett & Brassard, same era) as a key primitive for secure communication — later relevant because Shor's algorithm breaks classical encryption that QKD-based protocols can replace.
- **Feynman's motivation (early 1980s)** [~00:00]: simulating quantum systems on classical computers requires resources that grow exponentially with the number of particles, hitting an early infeasibility threshold; his proposal was to build computers that operate on quantum-physical principles instead. A Russian scientist proposed a similar idea around the same time, though Feynman's is the popularly cited origin.
- **CS-first framing of the course** [~00:00]: the workshop abstracts away physical hardware details and models classical/quantum computation mathematically, mirroring how early classical computer science (Turing, Church) modeled computation abstractly.
- **Turing machine & Church-Turing thesis** [~00:00]: the agreed-upon universal mathematical model of classical computation; "computable" = "computable by a Turing machine." Assumes unbounded memory in principle, but in practice memory/runtime are expressed as functions of input size n.
- **Extended Church-Turing thesis** [~00:00]: adds the requirement that different computational models can *efficiently* simulate one another (not just simulate at all). This efficiency requirement is the part that quantum computing (via Shor's algorithm) potentially falsifies.
- **Decision problems** [~00:00]: a computational problem is framed as: given an arbitrary binary string x ∈ {0,1}*, decide whether x belongs to a language L ⊆ {0,1}* (the set of "yes" instances).
- **Class P** [~00:00–20:00]: problems solvable by a deterministic algorithm in time polynomial in the input length n. Examples: primality testing, linear programming, graph connectivity / shortest path.
- **Class NP** [~20:00]: problems for which a candidate solution can be *verified* (not necessarily generated) in deterministic polynomial time. Illustrated via integer factorization — verifying candidate factors is just multiplication (fast), even though finding them may not be.
- **P vs NP** [~20:00–40:00]: the central open problem in theoretical CS; most researchers believe P≠NP but there's no proof; a Clay Millennium Prize ($1M) has gone unclaimed 25+ years; the problem traces back to a letter from Gödel to von Neumann.
- **NP-complete** [~40:00]: the hardest problems within NP (a subset of NP, under the P≠NP assumption). Factoring is in NP but *not* believed NP-complete — relevant because quantum computers are strongly believed unable to solve NP-complete problems in polynomial time, even though they beat classical computers on some NP problems (factoring).
- **Shor's algorithm & class BQP** [~40:00]: Peter Shor (early 1990s) showed integer factorization is solvable in polynomial time *on a quantum computer*. This defines the class **BQP** (Bounded-error Quantum Polynomial time). Crucial caveat: there's no proof that classical factoring requires exponential time, so quantum computing has not rigorously falsified the extended Church-Turing thesis — that requires an additional (still missing) classical lower-bound proof.
- **BQP vs NP vs NP-complete** [~40:00–80:00]: BQP is believed to contain parts of NP (factoring) but also to lie partly outside NP; NP-complete problems are strongly believed to lie outside BQP. Quantum computers trivially solve everything already in P (they can simulate classical computers).
- **NISQ era** [~60:00]: term proposed by John Preskill (Caltech), roughly early-to-mid 2010s, describing the current generation of noisy, non-fault-tolerant quantum devices accessible via the cloud (IBM, Rigetti, etc.) — shifting focus from purely theoretical research to hands-on experimentation (e.g., Bell-state experiments).
- **Search for "quantum advantage"** [~60:00]: framed as an evolving, back-and-forth race (analogous to cryptographic key-length arms races) rather than a fixed, permanent milestone — classical algorithms/hardware repeatedly catch up to claimed quantum speedups.
- **Hybrid algorithms** [~60:00]: the practical current-era approach — classical pre/post-processing combined with a quantum subroutine, typically applied to optimization or quantum machine learning (QML) problems.
- **De-quantization (Ewin Tang)** [~60:00–80:00]: a proposed quantum recommendation-system algorithm with a proven quantum speedup was later "de-quantized" by Ewin Tang, who found a classical algorithm matching its performance — a cautionary precedent when evaluating claimed quantum advantages, especially in QML.
- **Verification problem in the NISQ era** [~80:00]: for BQP problems that lie outside NP, there is no known efficient classical way to verify a quantum computer's output (e.g., ground-state finding / molecule simulation results) — contrasted with Shor's algorithm, whose output (factors) is efficiently verifiable by multiplication.
- **Three-way "sweet spot"** [~80:00]: the problems most relevant to quantum hackathons should ideally sit at the intersection of (1) in-principle quantum advantage, (2) efficient classical verifiability, and (3) implementability on current NISQ hardware — very few known problems satisfy all three; boson sampling and random circuit sampling satisfy (1)+(3) but not (2).

## Fórmulas y definiciones

- **Class P**: a problem is in P if a deterministic algorithm solves every instance in time bounded by a polynomial in the input length n (e.g., n², n³). [~20:00]
- **Class NP**: a problem is in NP if, given any candidate solution, its correctness can be verified by a deterministic algorithm in polynomial time. [~20:00]
- **NP-complete**: the subset of NP containing the problems believed to be the hardest in the class (assuming P≠NP). [~40:00]
- **BQP (Bounded-error Quantum Polynomial time)**: the class of problems solvable in polynomial time on a quantum computer. [~40:00]
- Decision-problem notation: input x ∈ {0,1}* (arbitrary-length binary string); language L ⊆ {0,1}* is the set of "yes" instances; solving the problem = deciding x ∈ L or x ∉ L. [~00:00–20:00]

## Glosario

- **Quantum money (Wiesner)**: 1970s proposal applying quantum physics to an information-theoretic problem (uncopyable currency). [~00:00]
- **QKD / BB84 (Bennett & Brassard)**: quantum protocol for secure cryptographic key sharing. [~00:00]
- **Turing machine**: the standard mathematical model of universal classical computation. [~00:00]
- **Church-Turing thesis / extended Church-Turing thesis**: equivalence of computational models; the extended version additionally requires that models can simulate each other *efficiently*. [~00:00]
- **P, NP, NP-complete, BQP**: complexity classes — see Fórmulas y definiciones above.
- **NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum)**: term coined by John Preskill for the current era of noisy, non-fault-tolerant quantum hardware. [~60:00]
- **Quantum advantage**: demonstrating that a quantum computer solves some well-defined problem better (time, memory, energy, or other resource) than any known classical approach — treated as an evolving race rather than a fixed milestone. [~60:00]
- **De-quantization (Ewin Tang)**: finding a classical algorithm that matches a quantum algorithm's performance, removing the need for quantum hardware. [~60:00–80:00]
- **Boson sampling / random circuit sampling**: NISQ-feasible problems (Scott Aronson; Google, respectively) with claimed in-principle quantum advantage but no known efficient classical verification of output. [~80:00]
