# Notes — Class 07

## Resumen

Sixth session in the instructor's own numbering, opening with reversible computation as a bridge concept, then building the oracle machinery (B_sub_f, phase kickback) needed for query-based quantum algorithms, deriving the n-qubit Hadamard identity, and culminating in a full geometric derivation of Grover's algorithm (rotation-matrix analysis, the O(√N) query bound, and the overshoot/undershoot caveat).

## Conceptos clave

- **Reversible transformations** [~00:00]: the classical AND gate (2 inputs, 1 output) is not reversible — the two output bits cannot be recovered from the single output. Made reversible by adding a third ancilla bit initialized to 0 that stores the AND result while the two original input bits pass through unchanged; this is a general technique — any function can be made reversible at the cost of extra memory (ancilla bits).
- **Aside: heat and irreversibility** [~00:00]: classical computers generate heat precisely because of this kind of irreversible information loss in ordinary digital logic (e.g., AND gates), motivating why reversible/unitary quantum operations are structurally different.
- **B_sub_f unitary (oracle construction)** [~00:00–20:00]: for a single-bit function f, defined on two qubits as |a⟩|b⟩ → |a⟩|b⊕f(a)⟩ — the first qubit a passes through unchanged, the second qubit b gets XORed with f(a). Shown to be self-inverse: applying B_f twice cancels the XOR, since two copies of f(a) parity-cancel (b⊕f(a)⊕f(a)=b).
- **Phase kickback** [~20:00]: setting the ancilla qubit to |−⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩−|1⟩) and applying B_f gives B_f|a⟩|−⟩ = (−1)^f(a)|a⟩|−⟩ — the value f(a) appears as a global sign/phase attached to |a⟩ rather than changing the ancilla's state at all; the ancilla remains unaffected and unentangled from the first qubit. Instructor states this only works with |−⟩ as the ancilla (not |+⟩) but does not give the full derivation of why. Instructor also made and immediately corrected a live explanation of whether B_f acts "inside" the tensor product, clarifying it's a single joint operation across the |a⟩|0⟩ and |a⟩|1⟩ branches, not two sequential steps.
- **n-qubit Hadamard identity** [~20:00–40:00]: derived step-by-step, starting from the single-qubit case H|a⟩ = (1/√2)Σ_{b∈{0,1}}(−1)^(a·b)|b⟩, extended to 2 qubits, then generalized to n qubits: H^⊗n|a⟩ = (1/√(2^n)) Σ_{b∈{0,1}^n} (−1)^(a·b)|b⟩, where a·b denotes the bitwise dot product (parity of the AND) between the two length-n binary strings. Explicitly flagged as the key building block for Grover's algorithm.
- **Historical chain, explicitly skipped in this course** [~20:00]: Deutsch's algorithm (the first quantum algorithm; David Deutsch also invented the quantum Turing machine concept) generalizes to Deutsch-Jozsa, then to Bernstein-Vazirani, then to Simon's algorithm, which historically led to Shor's algorithm. Grover's algorithm developed on a somewhat separate track; this course jumps directly to Grover without covering this oracle-algorithm lineage in detail.
- **Black-box/oracle model** [~20:00]: B_sub_f is treated as a given black box for the remainder of this derivation; how to actually construct B_f for a specific f is deferred to a later session ("tomorrow" per the transcript).
- **Grover's algorithm — problem statement** [~40:00]: unstructured search over f:{0,1}^n→{0,1}; goal is to find x such that f(x)=1. Contrasted with structured search (e.g., a sorted dictionary/binary search achieving O(log n) by exploiting order) — unstructured search has no such order to exploit.
- **Classical lower bound** [~40:00]: Ω(2^n) queries are required in the worst case classically, and this is proven optimal — brute-force search is the best possible classical algorithm for this problem.
- **Grover's quantum result** [~40:00]: O(√(2^n)) queries — a quadratic speedup over the classical bound. Explicitly noted this is proven optimal for quantum computers as well (unlike Shor's algorithm, where no classical lower bound has been proven, leaving the true size of the quantum-classical gap for factoring an open question).
- **Algorithm structure, 3 steps** [~40:00]: (1) apply H^⊗n to the initial state |0⟩^⊗n; (2) define the Grover operator G = H^⊗n · Z_0 · H^⊗n · Z_f and apply it exactly K times; (3) measure the n qubits.
- **Z_0 definition** [~40:00]: the identity matrix with its top-left entry flipped to −1, introducing a minus sign only on the |0...0⟩ basis state; in Dirac notation Z_0 = I − 2|0^n⟩⟨0^n|.
- **Z_f** [~40:00]: the oracle, implemented via the phase-kickback mechanism described above, expressed here in condensed notation that hides the explicit ancilla-qubit machinery.
- **Analysis technique — good/bad partition** [~40:00–60:00]: define set A ("good" strings, f(x)=1) with |A|=a, and set B ("bad" strings, f(x)=0) with |B|=b, where a+b=N=2^n. Define uniform superposition states |A⟩=(1/√a)Σ_{x∈A}|x⟩ and |B⟩=(1/√b)Σ_{x∈B}|x⟩ — mutually orthogonal since A and B are disjoint and exhaustive. Key simplification: the entire algorithm's evolution can be tracked using only the two coefficients on |A⟩ and |B⟩, instead of all 2^n individual amplitudes.
- **State after step 1** [~60:00]: |H⟩ = H^⊗n|0^n⟩ = √(a/N)|A⟩ + √(b/N)|B⟩.
- **Derivation of Z_f|A⟩ = −|A⟩** [~60:00]: every x∈A has f(x)=1, so (−1)^1=−1 applies uniformly across the whole |A⟩ superposition.
- **Derivation of G|A⟩** [~60:00]: using the identity H^⊗n Z_0 H^⊗n = I − 2|H⟩⟨H| (from Z_0's Dirac form together with H^⊗n|0^n⟩=|H⟩), combined with Z_f|A⟩=−|A⟩ cancelling the leading minus sign in G's definition, and computing ⟨H|A⟩=√(a/N), the result is: G|A⟩ = (1−2a/N)|A⟩ − (2√(ab)/N)|B⟩.
- **G|B⟩** [~60:00]: given directly (derivation left as an exercise): G|B⟩ = (2√(ab)/N)|A⟩ − (1−2b/N)|B⟩.
- **Matrix form as a rotation** [~60:00]: in the basis ordered {B,A}, G's action is encoded by the 2×2 matrix M = [[√(b/N), −√(a/N)],[√(a/N), √(b/N)]], recognized as equal to R(θ)² (a rotation matrix squared), where sin θ=√(a/N), cos θ=√(b/N) — visually identified as the same rotation-matrix structure introduced in earlier sessions (c05).
- **Consequence: G is a rotation by 2θ** [~60:00–80:00]: since M=R(θ)², a single application of G rotates by angle 2θ. Rewriting the initial state as |H⟩ = cos θ|B⟩ + sin θ|A⟩.
- **G^k|H⟩** [~80:00] = cos((2k+1)θ)|B⟩ + sin((2k+1)θ)|A⟩ — the "+1" term accounts for the rotation already embedded in |H⟩ before any G is applied; each additional application of G contributes a further 2θ rotation.
- **Stopping bound K** [~80:00]: choose K so that sin((2k+1)θ)≈1, i.e. (2k+1)θ≈π/2, giving K ≈ π/(4θ) − 1/2.
- **Single-marked-item case (a=1)** [~80:00]: θ = sin⁻¹(√(1/N)) ≈ √(1/N) using the small-angle approximation sin⁻¹(x)≈x, yielding K ≈ π√N/4 — confirming the O(√N)=O(√(2^n)) quadratic speedup over the classical O(2^n)/O(N) bound.
- **Overshoot/undershoot caveat** [~80:00]: Grover's algorithm is non-monotonic — applying G more times than the optimal K overshoots past alignment with |A⟩ and reduces the success probability; running the algorithm "longer" does not always help, contrary to typical intuitions about iterative refinement.
- **Deferred to next session** [~80:00]: an alternate, equivalent interpretation of the same algorithm as "inversion around the mean" (raised by a student, acknowledged by the instructor as valid, to be covered "at the start of the session tomorrow"), and the connection between Grover's algorithm and MaxCut (a combinatorial optimization problem), also promised for the following session.

## Fórmulas y definiciones

- **Reversible AND**: (a,b,0) → (a,b,a∧b) — ancilla bit stores the AND output; inputs pass through unchanged. [~00:00]
- **B_sub_f (oracle)**: |a⟩|b⟩ → |a⟩|b⊕f(a)⟩; self-inverse (B_f∘B_f = I). [~00:00–20:00]
- **Phase kickback**: B_f|a⟩|−⟩ = (−1)^f(a)|a⟩|−⟩, with |−⟩=(1/√2)(|0⟩−|1⟩). [~20:00]
- **Single-qubit Hadamard identity**: H|a⟩ = (1/√2)Σ_{b∈{0,1}}(−1)^(a·b)|b⟩. [~20:00–40:00]
- **n-qubit Hadamard identity**: H^⊗n|a⟩ = (1/√(2^n)) Σ_{b∈{0,1}^n} (−1)^(a·b)|b⟩, a·b = bitwise dot product/parity of AND. [~20:00–40:00]
- **Classical bound**: Ω(2^n) queries (proven optimal). [~40:00]
- **Grover bound**: O(√(2^n)) queries (proven optimal for quantum computers). [~40:00]
- **Grover operator**: G = H^⊗n · Z_0 · H^⊗n · Z_f. [~40:00]
- **Z_0**: I − 2|0^n⟩⟨0^n| (identity with top-left entry flipped to −1). [~40:00]
- **Good/bad superpositions**: |A⟩=(1/√a)Σ_{x∈A}|x⟩, |B⟩=(1/√b)Σ_{x∈B}|x⟩; a+b=N=2^n; ⟨A|B⟩=0. [~40:00–60:00]
- **Initial state**: |H⟩ = H^⊗n|0^n⟩ = √(a/N)|A⟩ + √(b/N)|B⟩ = cos θ|B⟩ + sin θ|A⟩, sin θ=√(a/N), cos θ=√(b/N). [~60:00]
- **Z_f on good/bad basis**: Z_f|A⟩ = −|A⟩. [~60:00]
- **H^⊗n Z_0 H^⊗n identity**: = I − 2|H⟩⟨H|. [~60:00]
- **G action**: G|A⟩ = (1−2a/N)|A⟩ − (2√(ab)/N)|B⟩; G|B⟩ = (2√(ab)/N)|A⟩ − (1−2b/N)|B⟩. [~60:00]
- **Matrix form**: M (basis {B,A}) = [[√(b/N), −√(a/N)],[√(a/N), √(b/N)]] = R(θ)². [~60:00]
- **k-fold application**: G^k|H⟩ = cos((2k+1)θ)|B⟩ + sin((2k+1)θ)|A⟩. [~80:00]
- **Optimal iteration count**: K ≈ π/(4θ) − 1/2; for a=1, K ≈ π√N/4. [~80:00]

## Glosario

- **Ancilla bit/qubit**: an auxiliary bit/qubit added to a computation, here initialized to 0 (classical) or |0⟩/|−⟩ (quantum), used to make a transformation reversible or to implement phase kickback. [~00:00–20:00]
- **B_sub_f**: the reversible/unitary oracle encoding a function f via XOR onto an ancilla qubit. [~00:00–20:00]
- **Phase kickback**: technique where a function's output is encoded as a phase on the input register rather than as a change to the ancilla. [~20:00]
- **a·b (bitwise dot product)**: parity of the bitwise AND between two length-n binary strings, used in the n-qubit Hadamard identity. [~20:00–40:00]
- **Unstructured search**: search problem with no exploitable order among candidates, as opposed to structured search (e.g., sorted lists). [~40:00]
- **Grover operator (G)**: the composite unitary H^⊗n·Z_0·H^⊗n·Z_f applied iteratively in Grover's algorithm. [~40:00]
- **Z_0, Z_f**: phase-flip operators — Z_0 flips the sign of |0^n⟩ only; Z_f flips the sign of states x with f(x)=1 (implemented via phase kickback). [~40:00–60:00]
- **|A⟩, |B⟩**: uniform superpositions over the "good" (f(x)=1) and "bad" (f(x)=0) computational basis states, respectively. [~40:00–60:00]
- **Inversion around the mean**: an alternate interpretation of Grover's algorithm mentioned by a student and acknowledged as equivalent, deferred to the next session. [~80:00]
- **Overshoot (Grover)**: applying G more times than the optimal K, which rotates past alignment with |A⟩ and reduces success probability. [~80:00]
