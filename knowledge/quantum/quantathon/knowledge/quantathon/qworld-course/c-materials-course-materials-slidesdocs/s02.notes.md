# Notes — Course materials (slides/docs), file 2

## Resumen

Second-module session on pitching, hosted by Tobias (Global Head of Science and Technology at Kai Ventures, a physicist-turned-VC firm based in Basel with offices in Singapore and Canada), moderated by Audrey. Covers why pitching matters, audience-awareness challenges specific to quantum/SDG hackathon pitches, the OQI-recommended pitch slide structure (title through next-steps), delivery/body-language advice, Q&A logistics (submission vs. pitch judging, team formation, hackathon selection process), and closes with a detailed walkthrough of a winning hackathon pitch (10Q, breast-cancer quantum machine learning, SDG 3) as a worked example of good storytelling structure.

## Conceptos clave

- **Speaker background and Kai Ventures** [~00:00]: Tobias, physicist by training, now Global Head of Science and Technology at Kai Ventures — an investor/ecosystem-builder working at the intersection of venture capital and quantum-startup translation from academia to industry; headquartered in Basel, Switzerland, with additional offices in Singapore (covering the broader APAC region, including a minor Tokyo presence) and Canada; also teaches entrepreneurship to students (with French/German partners) and has run hackathons with OQI (Geneva), plus separate hackathons in Calgary and Singapore focused on different SDGs.
- **Pitching across three career contexts** [~00:00]: academic pitching (to fellow physicists — technical depth appreciated, audience shares a common language); political pitching (to German government officials — audience wants the gist in one or two sentences, stops listening if over-elaborated); investor/industry pitching (now both giving pitches to companies and judging pitches from founders) — used to introduce the idea that pitching skills transfer across very different audiences despite surface differences.
- **Why pitching matters — three pillars** [~00:00]: (1) get people to invest in your idea (money, effort, or support); (2) find partners for your journey (since no one executes an idea alone); (3) get constructive feedback (clear communication is the foundation for precise feedback); plus, in the hackathon context specifically, pitching well helps you win.
- **Hackathon pitching challenges** [~00:00]: audiences are often not narrowly specialized in your exact topic even when technical; scientists tend to present "upside down" (background → methods → result) which frustrates non-technical audiences who want the result first; SDG relevance may not be the audience's default priority and must be actively justified.
- **Know the format** [~00:00]: strict time limits per hackathon; overshooting is heavily penalized (better to finish early than be cut off); Q&A length and feedback structure vary by event and should be learned in advance.
- **Pitching is fundamentally storytelling** [~00:00]: the goal is to engage and excite a human audience (investors, jury, politicians, scientists alike), not merely present neutral results; applies beyond formal pitches — even a person-to-person conversation to convince a friend is a "pitch."
- **Elevator pitch concept** [~00:00]: the idea of conveying key points in about 30 seconds (the time of an elevator ride) — described as a hard-to-master "gold standard" for extreme conciseness.
- **Audience-dependent structure ("pyramid" vs. "inverted pyramid")** [~00:00–20:00]: scientific/technical audiences tolerate a build-up-to-result structure; non-technical audiences expect results first, elaboration after (inverted pyramid) — the OQI hackathon format is explicitly recommended as a starting template, breakable only with conscious justification.
- **Slide/story-structure guidance** [~00:00–20:00]: define the problem and solution clearly so the quantum contribution's relevance is understood; slide headings should convey the main message on their own, since audiences can either read slides or listen, not both effectively; minimize on-slide text in favor of images/plots so the deck enhances rather than competes with the spoken narrative; make the problem "dramatic" to hook the audience before presenting the solution's excitement.
- **Delivery/communication advice** [~20:00]: speak clearly and concisely with short sentences (especially important when presenting in a non-native language, e.g. English); avoid turning your back to the audience to read slides — use printed/screen notes positioned so you can glance down without losing audience contact; strictly respect time limits (overshoot = penalty or being cut off, considered unfair to other participants); practice repeatedly with teammates.
- **Q&A preparation strategy** [~20:00]: anticipate likely jury questions and prepare backup slides for material that doesn't fit the main time slot; ideally structure the main pitch so it naturally provokes the prepared questions; different team members can specialize in different technical aspects and field corresponding Q&A questions.
- **Timing advice for hackathon teams** [~20:00]: don't leave pitch preparation to the last minute — assign one or two team members to start on it as early as the second day, running in parallel with the coding work.
- **Startup pitch structure vs. OQI hackathon pitch structure** [~20:00]: startup pitches (Kai Ventures' typical format) front-load vision → problem → solution in the first three slides, with everything after (market size, traction, etc.) serving to reinforce rather than re-establish the core story; OQI hackathon pitches follow an analogous logic — team intro, then problem, then solution at a surface level, before diving into technical depth — so the audience has a mental framework before encountering details.
- **Full OQI-recommended slide order** [~20:00]: (1) title — ideally a catchy "vision mission" phrase, not just the team name (Apple's "Think Different" cited as an example of extreme brevity); (2) team — backgrounds/expertise; (3) problem — which SDG, which specific metric/aspect targeted, who is affected, why the team chose it, and briefly why a quantum approach was considered given existing classical solutions; (4) solution — named, briefly described, framed in terms of broader intended scope beyond what 48 hours allowed to fully build; (5) classical baseline — how the problem is solved classically, whether the team built or reused an existing classical baseline, the benchmark metric used, and dataset references (can live on the slide without being spoken aloud); (6) quantum approach — justification for expecting quantum advantage, which algorithm was chosen and why, and which problem class it falls into; (7) hardware/simulator used; (8) implementation/results — GitHub link for reproducibility, transparency about single-shot vs. multiple runs, one primary results plot (classical vs. quantum curves) with clearly labeled axes, avoiding multiple simultaneous plots; (9) SDG impact — tie results back to the targeted SDG and speculate on broader applicability to other SDGs/problem classes; (10) next steps — publication plans, target audiences for follow-up, potential hardware-provider collaborations; (11) backup slides for the jury.
- **Recommendation on plot presentation** [~20:00]: show at most one plot at a time (or reveal plots sequentially via clicks) rather than presenting multiple plots simultaneously, since audiences get lost when forced to choose where to look.
- **Explaining quantum to non-technical audiences without oversimplifying** [~20:00–40:00]: focus on *what* the solution does rather than *how* quantum mechanics works underneath — illustrated with a drill analogy (people buy a drill for the hole it makes, not the drill itself); oversimplified quantum explanations risk spreading misconceptions (e.g., "the cat is alive and dead at the same time"); technical depth should be available on request but is not the main narrative focus for lay audiences.
- **Pitch length affects technical depth expected** [~20:00–40:00] (Audrey): longer pitch slots (e.g., 8 minutes) carry more expectation of technical elaboration; shorter slots require a stronger global-picture balance; specific hackathon hosts set their own expectations.
- **Submission vs. pitch are judged in parallel** [~20:00–40:00] (Audrey): the GitHub/technical submission (checked for reproducibility and honesty about what was actually delivered vs. promised) is judged alongside the live pitch; some hackathons pre-select based on submissions; teams should balance effort between the two rather than fully splitting into separate pitch/coding subteams (some mixing/communication recommended even when dividing tasks).
- **Jury preference for named algorithms/keywords** [~20:00–40:00] (Tobias): even in non-deep-technical pitches, jurors strongly value hearing the specific algorithm(s) used (classical and quantum) and a rough outline of pre-/post-processing steps, since full technical detail is reserved for the uploaded solution/GitHub repo that the jury reviews separately.
- **Team formation and hackathon status** [~20:00–40:00] (Audrey): this session is part of "pre-hackathon training"; attendees are only pre-selected, not yet officially confirmed for the hackathon itself — final selection depends on assessment performance (e.g., the "keyword assessment") and the need for geographically/field/level-diverse teams; team composition rules vary by specific regional hackathon and are set by local organizers, not by this general session; whether OQI/Kai Ventures hackathons continue annually depends on whether OQI's pilot phase (ending mid-2026, per s01) is extended into a 2027 continuation.
- **Worked example: winning pitch "10Q" (OQI × Kai Ventures hackathon, Geneva, October, SDG 3 — health)** [~40:00–60:00]: opened with a strong, immediate problem statement ("breast cancer is the most prevalent female cancer," with 10–30% of cases missed, cited with a scientific reference) rather than a team introduction; quickly established why classical AI falls short for this problem (data limited and data-deprived due to privacy policies) before introducing the team name; solution slide showed the pipeline visually (pre-processing → quantum part, naming amplitude encoding and a quantum convolutional neural network → prediction) before elaborating; alternated between explaining a method and immediately showing its corresponding result (rather than dumping all methods, then all results) — cited by the jury (including Tobias) as a specifically well-liked structural choice; included one notably weaker, equation-heavy slide, which the presenters still won despite ("nothing has to be perfect"); closed with a lighter "business" section (product/client/need) mirroring the "beyond current scope" idea from the slide-structure guidance, then a closing name/branding slide.
- **Secondary example referenced but not detailed here**: a separate, non-quantum, exactly-three-minute winning pitch from an "EOS hackathon" in London (blockchain-focused), mentioned as worth watching later for pacing/delivery inspiration but not walked through in this transcript. [~60:00]

## Glosario

- **Elevator pitch**: an extremely condensed (≈30-second) version of a pitch, delivered in the time it takes to share an elevator ride with someone. [~00:00]
- **Pyramid vs. inverted pyramid structure**: presentation ordering styles — pyramid builds up to the result (suited to technical/scientific audiences); inverted pyramid leads with the result before elaborating (suited to non-technical audiences). [~00:00–20:00]
- **Classical baseline**: the existing non-quantum method/benchmark against which a hackathon team's quantum approach is compared, including its metric and dataset references. [~20:00]
- **Keyword assessment**: a Canvas-based assessment used (per Audrey) as one selection criterion determining whether pre-selected trainees advance to official hackathon participation. [~40:00]
- **10Q**: the name of the example winning hackathon team/solution discussed in detail (breast-cancer quantum machine learning via amplitude encoding and a quantum convolutional neural network), from the OQI × Kai Ventures Geneva hackathon (SDG 3). [~40:00–60:00]
