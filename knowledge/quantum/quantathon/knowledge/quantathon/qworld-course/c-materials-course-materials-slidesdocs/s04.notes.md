## Conceptos clave

- **Speakers and roles** [~00:00]: Francesca Schiavello, OQI Quantum Applications Advisor, supports use-case teams with technical guidance on accelerating quantum computing applications for the SDGs; Alex Ben-Nasconi, project manager focused on impact, to present the impact side "tomorrow."
- **OQI's four strategic pillars ("four A's"), restated with slightly different framing than in c-materials s01** [~00:00]: A1 = accelerating applications for humanity (realizing quantum computing's potential for UN SDGs — Francesca and Alex's focus); A2 = access for all (partnering with quantum hardware providers and classical HPC providers to distribute access within the OQI community); A3 = advancing capacity building (Audrey's pillar — workshops, recorded knowledge, open-sourcing methodology, directly encompassing this very training session); A4 = activating multilateral governance (neutral forum shaping multilateral quantum governance for the SDGs).
- **OQI's internal use-case pipeline (A1)** [~00:00]: four phases spanning ideation to proof of concept over ~18 months, drawing on four expert types — quantum computing experts, classical computing experts, domain experts (validate that the problem is genuinely hard/challenging in its field), and impact experts (validate the problem is a genuine real-world need) — explicitly framed as multidisciplinary because "no one person can know everything."
- **OQI's four use-case method categories** [~00:00]: quantum simulation, quantum optimization, systems of equations/PDEs, and quantum machine learning — described as a non-exhaustive but representative taxonomy of OQI's use-case work.
- **Full OQI pipeline steps (used as the basis for hackathon compression)** [~00:00]: global challenge → SDG impact evaluation → mathematical/computational problem formulation → algorithm/methodology/code → quantum computing approach, further expanded to include benchmarking, resource estimation, scaling analysis, and noisy simulations (the last explicitly flagged as beyond hackathon scope given the tight timeline, but worth being aware of).
- **Compressed four-step hackathon workflow** [~00:00–20:00]: (1) identify the computational challenge (itself built on a prior, not-covered-here impact/SDG-validation step, deferred to Alex's session); (2) establish the classical baseline; (3) quantum implementation; (4) benchmarking — noting hackathons may let teams enter at different starting stages depending on the specific event, but understanding all four steps is still valuable.
- **Step 1 — identifying the computational challenge** [~00:00–20:00]: requires representing the SDG-connected problem mathematically, understanding classical solvability/intractability/bottlenecks and scaling behavior; strongly recommends grounding the mathematical formulation in existing literature rather than reinventing it; illustrated with a heat-diffusion/pipe-flow example solvable either via linear systems of equations or via a neural network (a PDE-to-ML reformulation trade-off, noted as more flexible but trickier to benchmark).
- **Step 2 — establishing the classical baseline** [~00:00–20:00]: choose a state-of-the-art classical algorithm from the literature (machine learning, linear systems, optimization, quantum simulation, etc.) and decide which success metric to prioritize (e.g., accuracy vs. speed) given known trade-offs and problem bottlenecks identified in step 1.
- **Step 3 — quantum implementation** [~00:00–20:00]: once bottlenecks/metrics are established, choose the quantum algorithm and hardware platform, with an explicit requirement to *justify* that choice (what theoretical property — e.g., better asymptotic scaling, better information storage — is being exploited), then handle implementation specifics (circuit design, hyperparameter tuning, classical pre-/post-processing) as time allows.
- **Step 4 — benchmarking, described as the session's biggest focus** [~20:00]: still an actively debated open question in the quantum computing community regarding best practices, both against classical algorithms and across different quantum platforms/hardware.
- **Fair-comparison principles for benchmarking** [~20:00]: (1) choose 1–2 clear metrics rather than trying to optimize everything simultaneously (common trade-off: accuracy vs. speed, illustrated via PDE numerical methods — Jacobi smoothing converges correctly but far slower than W-cycle/V-cycle multigrid methods; ML is a partial exception since more training doesn't always mean better results); (2) match the baseline's generality to the problem's generality (a general-purpose state-of-the-art method vs. a problem-specific one implies different, and harder-to-beat, comparison standards); (3) account for the full computational pipeline's cost, including classical pre-/post-processing (a quantum algorithm that looks efficient in isolation may be riding on a classically expensive preprocessing bottleneck, which would be an unfair comparison if ignored); (4) don't be discouraged that no quantum algorithm currently outperforms its classical counterpart at scale (Francesca states she hasn't found one yet) — rigorous testing and a well-justified, competitive algorithm is itself the achievement.
- **Statistical significance and avoiding cherry-picking** [~20:00]: a single favorable run is not sufficient evidence; illustrated with two overlapping performance distributions (quantum vs. classical) where selectively reporting only a lucky quantum-side outlier from the tail would misleadingly claim "quantum advantage" — multiple runs and reporting of averages/variance are required; robustness (tightness of the performance distribution) is itself a valid benchmarking metric, distinct from raw average performance (e.g., "worse on average but more consistent" is a legitimate finding); box-and-whisker or bell-curve style comparisons recommended.
- **Full-pipeline cost accounting example — QAOA for MaxCut** [~20:00–40:00]: worked example showing all post-processing steps (evaluating cost/metric per shot, storing/traversing all shots, selecting the best "alpha fraction" of outputs) — while noted that for typical shot counts (e.g., ~10,000 on IBM hardware) this classical post-processing is computationally trivial (fractions of a second), transpilation cost is comparatively significant and should be included in fair timing comparisons; broader message: every quantum algorithm is inherently hybrid (requires classical computation on both ends), so that classical cost is never negligible and must be reported.
- **CVaR (Conditional Value at Risk) as a benchmarking/selection tool** [~40:00]: rather than looking at the full shot distribution's average, CVaR focuses on the "alpha" tail-end fraction of best-performing shots — smaller alpha gives less consistent but potentially better best-case results, larger alpha gives more robust but less extreme results; relevant when reporting/benchmarking best-value outputs (best quantum shot vs. best classical value), and should itself be rerun multiple times and averaged; in Qiskit, the `Sampler` primitive exposes the full shot distribution (enabling CVaR-style tail analysis) while the `Estimator` primitive only returns the average, hiding the underlying shots.
- **Per-method benchmarking dataset guidance** [~40:00]: **MaxCut/graph optimization** — benchmark against a *class* of graphs sharing similar properties (size, average edges per node/density) rather than mixing heterogeneous random graphs, since performance varies strongly with graph density/sparsity; recommended escalation path: single instance repeated many times → a small set (e.g., 10) of same-class graphs → progressively larger/varied classes, to observe generalization and scaling; Gurobi cited as a common general-purpose classical optimization baseline, though problem-specific solvers may outperform it and are worth seeking out. **Machine learning** — use k-fold cross-validation (splitting one dataset into shuffled train/validation/test folds, e.g. k=5) rather than generating new datasets repeatedly; for quantum ML benchmarking specifically, either use a pre-trained/state-of-the-art classical model from the literature (recommended, given hackathon time constraints) or train your own classical model for a fair from-scratch comparison (costly in time). **Quantum simulation (e.g., VQE for ground-state energies)** — choose a diverse set of molecules with varied properties to avoid cherry-picking easy cases; use literature-reported classical ground-truth values (e.g., from DFT or CCSD) as the baseline rather than re-deriving them, since those classical methods are themselves computationally expensive. **Systems of equations/PDEs** — often highly non-linear, so averaging over multiple problem instances may be needed; genuine quantum scaling/compression advantages for this method class are expected to materialize mainly in the fault-tolerant quantum computing (FTQC) era, not necessarily on NISQ hardware today.
- **Scaling analysis and extrapolation** [~40:00]: validate algorithms first on small "toy scale" problems, then progressively test larger instances (bounded by available compute time/resources, e.g. simulating 50 qubits taking 6 hours vs. 30 qubits being more tractable); from results at (at least) three different scales, extrapolate a best-fit scaling curve for the quantum algorithm versus the classical baseline — enabling projections like "with 10,000 qubits, this becomes classically intractable to match," which is a valuable output even without present-day quantum advantage.
- **Use-case example: water leak detection (k-sensor placement)** [~40:00–60:00]: formulated as a Maximum Weighted Independent Set (MWIS) problem (selecting the maximum-weight set of mutually non-adjacent nodes in a graph); implemented on Pasqal's neutral-atom hardware specifically because the Rydberg blockade effect naturally enforces the "no adjacent nodes selected" constraint at the hardware level — cited as an example of well-justified algorithm/hardware co-selection; benchmarked against classical solvers (SciPy, Gurobi, HiGHS/"Hise") on synthetic datasets and real-world EPANET water-network files up to 450 nodes; resource-estimated up to 80 atoms (current hardware's practical limit); pipeline includes transforming the real water-distribution network into a hardware-compatible unit-disk graph, splitting it into subgraphs (max ~15 nodes per hardware constraint), solving MWIS per subgraph, and re-merging — with all these classical pre-/post-processing steps counted toward the fair-comparison cost accounting emphasized earlier.
- **Use-case example: molecular docking via boson sampling** [~40:00–60:00]: reformulates molecular docking (binding interactions of a "phenolacase" system, for extracting pollutants from water) as a graph-theoretic dense-subgraph problem; sits between machine learning and quantum simulation methodologically due to its classical post-processing; uses a boson-sampling approach on hardware from "Quinella" (name uncertain, see Huecos), chosen because that hardware type is specifically suited to this problem class; performed resource estimation identifying that classically intractable/poorly-performing regions occur for graphs larger than 600 nodes with density between ~0.3–0.5, and demonstrated favorable quantum scaling in that regime — but explicitly noted current quantum hardware isn't yet large enough (needs ~600 qubits) to tackle the real target problem, deferred to future hardware generations.
- **Use-case example: weather/climate forecasting via tensor-network compression** [~60:00]: uses quantum-inspired tensor networks to compress the relevant fields/dimensionality of PDEs used in climate/weather numerical schemes, compared against purely classical spectral-transform compression methods; explored from a theoretical perspective across various bond dimensions/compression levels, evaluating both accuracy and speed trade-offs.
- **Use-case example: forest degradation monitoring (hybrid quantum-classical ML)** [~60:00]: originally scoped as "deforestation monitoring," but reformulated after consultation with impact experts, who determined deforestation detection is already well-solved classically (a further 1% accuracy gain deemed not impactful) — illustrating the value of the impact-expert role in correctly scoping a problem; the revised use case targets forest *degradation* instead, using a hybrid approach with classical autoencoders to compress image data before quantum processing, motivated by current quantum hardware's limited input size capacity.

## Fórmulas y definiciones

- No closed-form mathematical formulas are stated verbatim in this transcript — the technical content is conceptual/methodological (benchmarking practice, use-case structuring), not equation-derivation based. [~00:00–80:00]
- **Maximum Weighted Independent Set (MWIS)**: selecting the maximum-weight subset of graph nodes such that no two selected nodes are adjacent (neighbors); used for k-sensor water-leak placement. [~40:00–60:00]
- **k-fold cross-validation**: splitting a single dataset into k shuffled train/validation(test) partitions (example given: k=5) to evaluate model robustness/generalization without generating new datasets repeatedly. [~40:00]
- **CVaR (Conditional Value at Risk), applied to shot distributions**: restricting evaluation to the best alpha-fraction tail of a distribution of sampled algorithm outputs (shots), rather than the full-distribution average; smaller alpha → less consistent but potentially better best-case outcomes; larger alpha → more robust, less extreme outcomes. [~40:00]
- **Scaling extrapolation**: fitting a best-fit curve to algorithm performance measured at (at least) three problem scales (toy → intermediate → largest tested), to project performance/advantage at scales beyond what could be directly tested (e.g., at 10,000-qubit hardware). [~40:00]

## Glosario

- **Four A's (OQI strategic pillars)**: A1 accelerating applications for humanity, A2 access for all, A3 advancing capacity building, A4 activating multilateral governance. [~00:00]
- **Domain expert / impact expert / classical computing expert / quantum computing expert**: OQI's four collaborating expertise roles in use-case development — domain experts validate a problem's genuine difficulty within its field; impact experts validate the problem's real-world relevance/need; classical and quantum computing experts handle the respective algorithmic/implementation sides. [~00:00]
- **Baseline / benchmark**: the reference classical algorithm and its chosen success metric(s), against which a quantum approach's performance is fairly compared. [~00:00–20:00]
- **Fair comparison (in benchmarking)**: a comparison accounting for the full computational pipeline's cost (including classical pre-/post-processing), using an appropriately general or specific state-of-the-art baseline, and reporting statistically robust (multi-run, non-cherry-picked) results. [~20:00]
- **Robustness (of an algorithm's performance)**: the tightness/consistency of an algorithm's output distribution across repeated runs, usable as a benchmarking metric distinct from average performance. [~20:00]
- **CVaR (Conditional Value at Risk)**: metric/selection strategy focusing on the best-performing tail fraction of a sampled distribution rather than its full average, relevant to optimizing/reporting quantum-algorithm shot outputs. [~40:00]
- **Rydberg blockade effect**: physical mechanism in neutral-atom quantum hardware (e.g., Pasqal) that suppresses simultaneous excitation of nearby atoms, naturally enforcing spatial/graph-adjacency constraints — exploited here to encode the MWIS "no adjacent nodes" constraint directly in hardware. [~40:00–60:00]
- **Boson sampling**: a quantum computing approach based on sampling from photonic/bosonic systems, used here (on hardware referred to as "Quinella," spelling uncertain) for a molecular-docking use case reformulated as a dense-subgraph problem. [~40:00–60:00]
- **FTQC (fault-tolerant quantum computing)**: the future hardware era, distinct from today's NISQ devices, in which certain algorithm classes (e.g., for PDEs/systems of equations) are expected to realize their theoretical scaling advantages. [~40:00]
