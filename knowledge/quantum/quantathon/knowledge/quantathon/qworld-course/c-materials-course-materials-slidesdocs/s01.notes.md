# Notes — Course materials (slides/docs), file 1

## Resumen

Opening session of the hackathon's "second module," on responsible quantum computing and impact, led by Mira (Open Quantum Institute, A4 pillar — diplomat engagement/quantum governance) with Audrey moderating. Covers the rapid recent progress in quantum computing (2025 investment/Nobel Prize milestones, Google's data-processing advantage claim), the Collingridge dilemma of when to regulate a new technology, definitions and key tension areas of "responsible quantum computing" from the literature, the quantum divide (UNESCO study statistics), the history/mission/four pillars of the Open Quantum Institute (OQI), and closes with an audience Q&A on national strategy, governance timing, and career opportunities. This is not qworld-course technical/algorithmic content — it is a policy/ethics/governance talk associated with the hackathon prep track, filed here as course material.

## Conceptos clave

- **Session framing / module structure** [~00:00]: this is the first of the "second module" (post pre-hackathon technical training), focused on responsible quantum computing and impact; later sessions in this module cover pitching (with Kai Ventures, May 28–29) and quantum computing applications for the UN Sustainable Development Goals (June 4–5), presented by the OQI use-case team.
- **Rapid quantum computing progress (2025 timeline)** [~00:00]: NVIDIA CEO Jensen Huang's January 2025 remark that quantum computers are "20 years away" tanked quantum stocks, followed by a March correction after quantum-company CEOs met with him, and a June reversal saying interesting problems could be solved "in the coming years"; over $2B invested in quantum hardware companies (IQM, QuEra, PsiQuantum, Quantinuum named) around September 2025; 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics awarded to John Clarke, Michel Devoret, and John Martinis for superconducting-qubit advances.
- **Google's "Exponential Quantum Advantage in Processing Massive Classical Data" paper (April 2025)** [~00:00]: claims ~60 qubits can process a dataset a million times larger than classical computers could; connected to Shor's algorithm risk timelines; Google reportedly decided not to publish the underlying algorithms further, citing fear of misuse.
- **The two-futures framing** [~00:00]: a simplified choice between a beneficial future (quantum computers solving climate/carbon-capture materials design, faster drug discovery, antibiotic resistance) versus a "doom and gloom" future (breaking encryption, a 2021 Wired headline "the quantum apocalypse is coming, be very afraid") — presented as a societal choice, not a binary in practice.
- **Collingridge dilemma** [~00:00]: general dilemma for any new technology — the "control curve" and "predictability curve" intersect at an ideal-intervention point, but it's hard to know in real time when that point has arrived, and hard to predict quantum computing's eventual uses since new use cases are invented as the technology diffuses.
- **Historical framing via Heisenberg** [~00:00]: reference to Werner Heisenberg's book "Quantum Physics and Philosophy" (German-language original), where he reflects (from British captivity, post-atomic-bomb) on scientists' responsibility, noting his colleagues might have built the same weapon had timing/orders differed — used to motivate integrating ethical questions from the start rather than in hindsight.
- **Responsible Research and Innovation (RRI) framework** [~00:00]: pillars of anticipation, reflexivity, inclusion, and responsiveness, plus values like being human-centric, efficient, transparent, accessible — meant to be integrated into innovation processes from the start, not retrofitted.
- **Science anticipation / "honest brokering"** [~00:00–20:00]: the speaker's affiliation (Geneva Science and Diplomacy Anticipator, GESDA) works to inform diplomats early about scientific developments via neutral, non-evaluative information exchange ("honest brokering"), enabling policymakers (who may feel science "isn't for them") to engage and act on the Collingridge dilemma before diffusion makes intervention harder.
- **Definitions of "responsible quantum computing"** [~20:00]: (1) from the ResQT community (led by "Zeki," running an annual conference) — "quantum technology developments that align with ethical principles, promote equity, and mitigate unintended consequences"; (2) the speaker's own IBM-era definition — "quantum computing that is aware of the power of its effects" / "not being naive" (e.g., knowing what a defense-industry client's products do); (3) a critique from other colleagues that AI-ethics frameworks are a useful starting point but insufficient for quantum-specific ethical challenges, since quantum's eventual use cases aren't yet fully known.
- **Main topic areas of responsible quantum (recommended "screenshot" slide)** [~20:00]: policy (international cooperation/security balance, societal involvement), legal (openness vs. IP protection, export controls — driven largely by US restrictions on trade with China/Russia, regulation/standardization timing), ethical ("for good" vs. military/dual-use ambiguity, quantum computers' actual energy efficiency, integrating RRI into quantum research/education), societal (inclusion/diversity, the quantum divide, democratizing access).
- **Key tensions** [~20:00–40:00]: open science vs. national security (e.g., European sovereignty debates, defense against Shor's-algorithm risk); investment-map disparity — countries already advanced in classical tech also dominate quantum investment, reinforcing rather than closing the divide.
- **Quantum-safe cryptography / "harvest now, decrypt later"** [~20:00–40:00]: quantum computers threaten to break most current encryption (via Shor's algorithm); adversaries may be collecting encrypted data now (health, financial, national-security records) to decrypt once a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists; classical quantum-safe/post-quantum algorithms already exist, but awareness of the need to adopt them is the main gap, especially outside well-resourced institutions.
- **The quantum divide — UNESCO study statistics** [~20:00–40:00]: only 29 countries have a national quantum strategy; investment/strategy concentrated in already-advanced nations; 1 in 3 researchers surveyed lack access to quantum facilities despite wanting it; 84% of countries report talent drain or stagnation; women make up only 20% of the quantum workforce — explicitly compared to the unresolved analogous divide from classical computing, framed as a chance to "not repeat the mistake."
- **Open Quantum Institute (OQI) — mission and history** [~40:00]: "a multilateral governance initiative that brings together academia, industry, and diplomacy to promote global inclusive access to quantum computing and the development of applications for the benefit of humanity [and the planet]"; conceived 2021 (via GESDA's anticipation "radar" identifying the emerging quantum divide), publicly announced 2022, structural setup phase through 2024, launched with CERN as host institution and funded initially by UBS (Swiss bank) for a pilot phase; pilot phase concluding mid-2026, hoping to enter a post-pilot phase in 2027.
- **Why CERN as host** [~40:00]: CERN's culture of technology-sharing with society, nonprofit/open-science ethos (invention of the World Wide Web cited), history of international collaboration continuing through hard geopolitical periods (e.g., the Cold War) — presented as strongly mission-aligned with OQI.
- **OQI's four pillars** [~40:00]: A1 = applications for humanity (UN-sourced real-world challenge use cases, vetted for quantum relevance, paired subject-matter experts with computing experts); A2 = access for all (redistributing donated quantum computing resources); A3 = education (hackathons, "hackathon in a box," training, led by Audrey — the pillar most directly relevant to this workshop); A4 = multilateral governance / diplomat engagement for the SDGs (led by the speaker, Mira).
- **A3 target groups and tools** [~40:00–60:00]: undergraduate/graduate CS-physics-engineering students; emerging regional quantum hubs; diplomats/policymakers — served via hackathons, a quantum diplomacy game, and an education consortium of 80+ experts; described as a "pyramid" from skill development to ecosystem building to SDG applications. Countries newly engaged (beyond the earlier investment-map disparity) include Mexico, Costa Rica, Brazil, Morocco, Palestine, Egypt, UAE, Pakistan, Vietnam, Jordan, Kenya.
- **OQI's call to action** [~40:00–60:00]: promote sustainable/low-ecological-footprint quantum R&D practices; proactively mitigate quantum security risk; prioritize applications benefiting humanity/the planet (framed as compatible with, even reinforcing, commercial success); champion openness, interdisciplinarity, diversity, equitable access; support pragmatic governance frameworks that don't hinder innovation. Industry examples of adjacent commitments cited: IBM's 2024 published responsible quantum principles, World Economic Forum responsible quantum governance principles, a Microsoft blog post on responsible computing.
- **Personal-action closing advice** [~40:00–60:00]: stay curious, ask questions (even to non-ethics-trained professors), reflect critically on career/research choices aligning with personal values, talk about these questions publicly, join responsible-quantum communities, and remain vocal even when such efforts face pushback.
- **Q&A highlights** [~40:00–80:00]: (1) balancing national strategic interest vs. international collaboration — argued for more collaboration under tension, not less, finding aligned-values partners; (2) responsibility/governance should be integrated "at every stage" of quantum work — including hardware (e.g., helium-3/helium-4 coolant supply tied to nuclear-material production) and theory (e.g., a drug-discovery algorithm's potential dual-use as a toxin-design tool); (3) job opportunities are mainly through OQI's local-ecosystem/regional-ambassador model and hackathon-organizer networking, since the 2026 pilot-phase "pipeline is full" but future calls are expected; (4) concrete next steps for Global South national quantum strategies — join the Global Quantum Initiative (UNESCO + partners, successor to the concluded International Year of Quantum), and consult World Economic Forum / OECD analyses of existing national strategies.

## Glosario

- **Responsible quantum computing (RQC)**: the practice of developing quantum technology in alignment with ethical principles, equity, and mitigation of unintended consequences; multiple competing definitions exist since the field is young. [~20:00]
- **Collingridge dilemma**: general tech-policy dilemma about the optimal timing of regulatory intervention, balancing predictability of harms against the technology's degree of diffusion/control. [~00:00]
- **Honest brokering**: a neutral, non-evaluative mode of exchanging scientific information with policymakers/diplomats, used by GESDA in "science anticipation" work. [~00:00–20:00]
- **Quantum divide**: the disparity in quantum computing investment, strategy, talent, and access between technologically advanced and less-advanced nations — explicitly likened to an unresolved analogous divide in classical computing. [~20:00–40:00]
- **Harvest now, decrypt later**: the security risk pattern where encrypted data is collected today with the expectation of future decryption once cryptographically relevant quantum computers exist. [~20:00–40:00]
- **Open Quantum Institute (OQI)**: a multilateral governance initiative (hosted at CERN, initially funded by UBS) promoting global inclusive access to quantum computing and applications for humanity/the planet, structured around four pillars (A1–A4). [~40:00]
- **GESDA (Geneva Science and Diplomacy Anticipator)**: the organization (referenced as "Jester" phonetically in the transcript) that connects scientists and diplomats via anticipation/honest-brokering methodology, and originated the idea for OQI. [~00:00, ~40:00]
- **Global Quantum Initiative**: a UNESCO-and-partners initiative continuing the goals of the concluded International Year of Quantum, recommended as a concrete next step for countries formulating national quantum strategies. [~60:00–80:00]
