- **Recap of the winning "10Q" breast-cancer pitch** [~20:00–40:00]: same example as s02 — immediate problem framing (breast cancer prevalence, 10–30% missed cases, human error), classical AI's limitations (small/private datasets) motivating the quantum approach, concise team/solution presentation (pre-processing → amplitude encoding → quantum convolutional neural network → prediction), benchmarked accuracy beating the classical counterpart for all but one epoch, one overloaded/math-heavy slide singled out as the pitch's weakest point (poorly received by the jury, understood only in later one-on-one conversation) yet the team still won — reinforced as encouragement that imperfection doesn't preclude winning; also tried angle encoding with different preprocessing as an alternative ansatz, presented as a benchmarking comparison; included a hackathon-specific "business" slide (product/customer/need) not expected to appear in the attendees' own hackathons.
- **Honestly presenting non-advantageous quantum results** [~40:00]: explicit answer to a question about pitching a conceptually promising but experimentally non-beating-classical quantum approach — reassures that no current quantum solution outperforms classical methods at scale given today's hardware constraints; recommends presenting real results honestly and, if possible, identifying a trend suggesting future scaling advantage (e.g., via emulator projections with more qubits or lower error rates) rather than overstating current performance.
- **Handling an unanswerable jury question** [~40:00]: recommended response is to openly acknowledge not knowing the answer, then think aloud for ~10–20 seconds about how one *would* investigate it, or offer to follow up later — framed as more credible than guessing or mumbling.
- **Device and presentation-format logistics** [~40:00]: two common hackathon setups — presenters use their own laptop, or (organizer-preferred) all slides are collected beforehand onto a single shared device to avoid cross-platform/OS compatibility issues; PDF and PowerPoint formats are described as universally safe, while unusual formats (e.g., Canva) risk technical failures on a shared device — cited as having caused real problems at past Kai Ventures hackathons.
- **Most common pitching mistakes (per the speaker's experience)** [~40:00]: (1) facing the screen/wall instead of the audience; (2) over-explaining technical detail in a short pitch — reiterating that "a pitch is not a lecture," full step-by-step methodology belongs in backup slides, shown only if asked; (3) overshooting allocated time, often from over-elaborating a particular slide — mitigated by setting internal time checkpoints per slide during rehearsal so pacing can be corrected mid-pitch.
- **Evaluation criteria are hackathon-specific** [~40:00]: the speaker declines to generalize hackathon evaluation weighting (pitch vs. GitHub repo/demo vs. documentation), deferring entirely to each local hackathon's organizers, who typically clarify this at the event's start and via mentors.
- **What judges love to see** [~40:00–60:00]: generating genuine audience excitement — vocal engagement, reading the room, personality/humor if natural (forced humor "never works") — described as having outsized impact beyond content alone.
- **Framing current quantum hardware limitations positively** [~60:00]: rather than downplaying hardware limits, explicitly quantify them (e.g., "doesn't work with only 30 qubits, but would with 60") as a forward-looking research trajectory — reframes the limitation as evidence the team understands next steps and that the solution's value will grow as hardware improves, rather than being an inherent flaw of the solution itself.
- **Team formation logistics** [~60:00]: deferred to individual hackathon organizers (pre-formed via Discord/Slack, or spontaneous on-site formation, or a mixture, as at Kai Ventures' own events); from the speaker's personal experience, solo participants essentially never win — teams provide motivation and sparring; ideal team size (personal opinion, not an OQI rule) is roughly 3–5 people — below 3, workload concentrates on too few people; above 5, some members tend to disengage (school-project analogy).
- **Technique for anticipating jury questions ("reverse engineering" the pitch)** [~60:00]: build the presentation with an intended answer to an anticipated question, then deliberately remove part of that answer from the final slides, leaving a natural "gap" the audience is primed to ask about — explicitly flagged as difficult and risky (can look like incomplete preparation) and best refined by rehearsing in front of test audiences and noting which questions recur across listeners; suggested to start rehearsing with audiences similar to your own background (whose likely questions mirror your own) before attempting this with less familiar audiences.
- **Mid-hackathon project pivoting** [~60:00–80:00]: whether project changes are allowed mid-hackathon depends on the hackathon's structure — open-choice hackathons (participants pick their own problem) are generally more flexible than organizer-assigned-problem hackathons, where switching may be blocked only by logistical constraints like a capped number of teams per problem (tied to limited hardware credits per problem). Practical advice: only switch on day one if at all; switching on day two typically causes fatal time pressure within the ~48-hour (Friday–Sunday) window; prefer iterating within the same core project (e.g., trying an alternative algorithm/encoding) over abandoning it, which can even become part of the pitch as a benchmarking comparison (as the winning "10Q" team did by trying three different encodings).
